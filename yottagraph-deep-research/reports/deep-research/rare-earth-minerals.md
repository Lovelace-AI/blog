# What is the supply chain risk landscape for rare earth minerals critical to AI hardware and defense, and which producers and geopolitical actors hold the most leverage?

## Executive Summary

*   The global supply chain for rare earth elements remains structurally vulnerable and highly concentrated, granting immense geopolitical leverage to a single state actor, the People's Republic of China (https://anderseninstitute.org/chinas-export-control-architecture-and-its-use-of-critical-minerals-as-strategic-pressure-points/).
*   Evidence suggests that artificial intelligence hardware and advanced defense systems face severe material bottlenecks, particularly regarding heavy rare earths required for thermal stability (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom).
*   It seems likely that Western state-sponsored interventions, such as those executed by the United States Department of Defense, will require years of sustained capital deployment to meaningfully counterbalance existing market monopolies (https://fas.org/publication/unpacking-dod-and-mp-partnership/).
*   The fundamental leverage in the market is held by the China Rare Earth Group, a state-owned enterprise controlling approximately 62 percent of China's heavy rare earth supplies (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/).
*   Western producers attempting to break this monopoly, specifically MP Materials and Lynas Rare Earths, are entirely dependent on novel government subsidies and price floor guarantees to survive predatory pricing dynamics (https://mpmaterials.com/news/mp-materials-announces-transformational-public-private-partnership-with-the-department-of-defense-to-accelerate-u-s-rare-earth-magnet-independence/).

The integration of rare earth minerals into advanced computational hardware and next-generation military defense systems has elevated an obscure metallurgical niche into a premier theater of modern geopolitical competition. While historical commodity markets were driven primarily by the basic industrial needs of construction and energy, the contemporary supply chain for critical minerals is inextricably linked to technological supremacy. The evidence leans toward a reality where the transition to artificial intelligence (AI) and the maintenance of modern military readiness cannot be achieved without secure access to specific lanthanide series elements. However, building resilient supply chains is not merely a matter of discovering unmined deposits; it requires mastering highly toxic, capital-intensive chemical separation processes that have been consolidated over decades by adversarial geopolitical actors.

This comprehensive research report analyzes the intricate supply chain risk landscape for rare earth minerals critical to AI and defense applications. It systematically explores the market overview, supply and demand landscapes, the positioning of key producers, overarching macroeconomic risks, and the forward-looking price outlook. By examining the public-private partnerships emerging in the West and the rapid consolidation of state-owned enterprises in the East, this document maps the leverage points that will define the global technology and defense sectors throughout the late 2020s.

## 1. Market Overview

The market for rare earth elements operates at the nexus of geology, advanced chemistry, and national security policy. Unlike traditional base metals such as copper or iron, which are traded in highly liquid, transparent global markets, rare earths are characterized by opaque pricing mechanisms, heavily restricted export quotas, and a supply chain architecture designed to capture value-added manufacturing rather than raw material export. The strategic criticality of these minerals stems not from their absolute scarcity in the Earth's crust, but from the extreme technical difficulty and environmental hazards associated with separating the tightly bound elements from one another.

To understand the market overview, one must first establish the scale and structural dependencies that define current global consumption. The United States Geological Survey (USGS) plays a critical role in forecasting the risks that supply chain disruptions pose to both economic stability and national security (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2024/mcs2024-rare-earths.pdf).

*   In 2025, the USGS estimated that world mine production of rare earth elements reached 390,000 tons of rare-earth-oxide (REO) equivalent (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf).
*   This represents a staggering increase from historical baselines; for context, in 1994, global production was merely 64,500 tons (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2024/mcs2024-rare-earths.pdf).
*   The estimated leading domestic end use of rare earths in the United States was catalysts, whereas the estimated leading global use was magnets (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf).
*   A significant amount of imported rare earths consumed by Western economies is embedded invisibly within finished manufactured goods (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf).

The synthesis of this market data reveals a profound structural vulnerability for Western economies. While the United States possesses domestic mining capabilities—evidenced by the 51,000 tons of rare-earth-oxide (REO) equivalent produced domestically in 2025—the nation remains critically dependent on international actors for the refined compounds and metals that are actually integrated into finished technologies (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf). The data indicates that U.S. imports of rare-earth compounds and metals increased by 169 percent in 2025 (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf). However, the overall estimated value of these imports slightly decreased to $165 million from $168 million in the prior year, reflecting a structural shift toward lower-value imported intermediate products rather than high-purity metals (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf).

Furthermore, the domestic mining output is heavily skewed toward light rare earth elements (LREEs), such as cerium and lanthanum, rather than the heavily coveted heavy rare earth elements (HREEs) (https://mpmaterials.com/news/mp-materials-announces-transformational-public-private-partnership-with-the-department-of-defense-to-accelerate-u-s-rare-earth-magnet-independence/). Heavy rare earths, which include atomic numbers corresponding to elements like terbium, dysprosium, and lutetium, are far less abundant but absolutely critical for high-temperature applications in defense and AI data centers (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom). The USGS confirms that the United States has a 100 percent net import reliance for heavy rare earth compounds and metals (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2024/mcs2024-rare-earths.pdf). This absolute dependency is the foundational vulnerability of the market, setting the stage for aggressive geopolitical maneuvering.

### Financial and Market-Structure Analysis

The financial architecture of the rare earth market is highly anomalous compared to other commodity sectors. Because a significant amount of imported rare earths is embedded within finished manufactured goods, the true economic footprint of the industry is vastly larger than the raw material valuation suggests (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf). The raw minerals themselves represent only a fraction of the value chain, functioning as the narrow tip of a massive economic spear.

In a traditional commodity market, sustained high prices theoretically incentivize new market entrants to discover and develop new mineral deposits, eventually leading to increased supply and price stabilization. However, the rare earth market experiences a persistent "processing bottleneck." Extracting the raw ore is only the first, and arguably the simplest, step. The true financial moat lies in the complex metallurgical processes required to separate these chemically similar elements from one another.

To understand solvent extraction methodologies, one must envision the process of separating mixed liquids of almost identical densities; it requires an intricate, continuous fluid dynamics system where hundreds of sequential mixer-settler stages use slightly varying chemical affinities to slowly coax individual elements apart over weeks of continuous processing.

*   The purified rare earth solution must enter a multi-stage counter-current solvent extraction circuit comprising 50 to 200 distinct mixer-settler stages (https://www.procurementresource.com/reports/praseodymium-manufacturing-plant-project-report).
*   The capital expenditure (CAPEX) required to build these solvent extraction facilities ranges drastically from $0.016 to $608 per kilogram of REE produced, depending on scale and environmental compliance (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914704/).
*   The operational expenditure (OPEX) for these separation facilities is similarly broad and punitive, ranging from $0.91 to $11,000 per kilogram of REE produced (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914704/).
*   On average, the selective separation and refining steps comprise the vast majority of processing costs, accounting for 67 percent of the total cost from mining through final refining (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914704/).
*   In typical operations, reagents and consumable chemicals account for approximately 85 percent of total ongoing processing costs (https://ramacoresources.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Ramaco-PEA-Report-Jul-2025.pdf).

Turning raw minerals into usable metals is immensely capital-intensive, highly toxic, and time-consuming, effectively creating a structural barrier to entry that deters private capital and prevents rapid supply responses to demand shocks. Consequently, the market structure remains stubbornly monopolistic, requiring immense public-sector capital interventions to alter the financial calculus for Western producers.

## 2. Supply Landscape

The supply landscape for rare earths is the primary locus of risk for AI and defense hardware developers. Unlike oil or agricultural products, which feature distributed global production bases, the rare earth supply chain is heavily centralized. This centralization is not entirely the result of geological distribution—rare earths are relatively abundant in the Earth's crust globally—but rather the result of decades of targeted industrial policy, environmental arbitrage, and state-sponsored consolidation by the People's Republic of China (https://repository.mines.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/cc3275df-880d-4de0-9384-bcafa0f13d72/content).

*   China is responsible for approximately 70 percent of global rare earth production (https://repository.mines.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/cc3275df-880d-4de0-9384-bcafa0f13d72/content).
*   China accounts for nearly 90 percent of the processing and refining of global rare earth output (https://rareearthexchanges.com/news/china-rare-earth-group-co-ltd/).
*   China controls 94 percent of global rare earth element sintered permanent magnet production (https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/88d2b060-4c5b-46cc-8727-c60576cb937d/RareearthelementsPathwaystosecureanddiversifiedsupplychains.pdf).
*   Until 2023, China accounted for 99 percent of global heavy rare earth processing (https://www.csis.org/analysis/consequences-chinas-new-rare-earths-export-restrictions).
*   Currently, only an estimated 10 percent of total global rare earth separation capacity exists outside the borders of China (https://rare-earth-mining.com/rare-earth-market-june-2026/).
*   The United States produces zero domestic gallium, a critical semiconductor material largely controlled by China (https://anderseninstitute.org/chinas-export-control-architecture-and-its-use-of-critical-minerals-as-strategic-pressure-points/).

The profound concentration of processing infrastructure in Asia means that even allied nations with mining capacity are functionally dependent on Chinese refining operations. Historically, Western mining operators have been forced to send their unseparated mineral concentrates to China for processing, as domestic facilities capable of managing the complex chemical solvent extraction methodologies did not exist at scale (https://repository.mines.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/cc3275df-880d-4de0-9384-bcafa0f13d72/content).

This dynamic was severely weaponized in 2025. On April 4, 2025, China actively tightened its export controls on rare-earth elements, introducing specific national-security-based licensing requirements and restrictions on the export of specific heavy rare earth alloys, compounds, metals, and oxides (https://www.csis.org/analysis/consequences-chinas-new-rare-earths-export-restrictions).

The targeted elements were highly specific and designed to cripple downstream high-tech manufacturing. The restrictions applied to seven specific heavy rare earths: samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium (https://www.csis.org/analysis/consequences-chinas-new-rare-earths-export-restrictions). By October 2025, these controls were further expanded to encompass five additional heavy elements: holmium, erbium, thulium, europium, and ytterbium (https://orfamerica.org/orf-america-comments/chinas-rare-earth-export-restrictions-triggered-diversification). Although Beijing temporarily suspended the October controls in November 2025 for a period of one year, the original April restrictions remained strictly in effect, drastically reducing global supply liquidity (https://www.moomoo.com/community/feed/weekly-strategy-report-u-s-rare-earths-2026-mp-has-116522130735909).

To understand the granular impact of the April 2025 export restrictions, one must examine the specific end-uses and supply vulnerabilities of the heavily targeted, yet lesser-discussed elements: Samarium, Gadolinium, and Scandium.

*   **Samarium:** Targeted specifically as metallic samarium and samarium-containing alloys, such as samarium-cobalt (SmCo) alloys, which are critical for specialized aerospace magnets that must endure extreme demagnetizing fields (https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/china-imposes-export-controls-on-medium-and-heavy-rare-earth-materials).
*   **Gadolinium:** Targeted as metallic gadolinium and gadolinium-magnesium alloys, which are essential for advanced nuclear reactor shielding and specialized medical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents (https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/china-imposes-export-controls-on-medium-and-heavy-rare-earth-materials).
*   **Scandium:** Targeted as metallic scandium and scandium-aluminum alloys, which provide unparalleled strength-to-weight ratios necessary for next-generation military aerospace frames and advanced fuel cells (https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/china-imposes-export-controls-on-medium-and-heavy-rare-earth-materials).

A critical synthesis of this supply landscape reveals that "diversification" on paper often masks deeper structural dependencies in reality. For instance, while shipping records might indicate that the United States imports certain heavy rare earth compounds from nations like Germany, Japan, the Republic of Korea, and the Netherlands, these materials are functionally derived from mineral concentrates and chemical intermediates that were originally processed elsewhere—predominantly in China (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2024/mcs2024-rare-earths.pdf). Therefore, an export control enacted in Beijing immediately cascades through European and allied supply chains, ultimately starving U.S. defense contractors and AI hardware developers of essential inputs.

### Business-Model Analysis of the Supply Chain

The traditional business model for Western rare earth companies has been inherently flawed when viewed through the lens of national security. Historically, companies operated purely as extraction entities (miners), pulling ore from the ground and selling the mixed concentrate into the global spot market (https://repository.mines.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/cc3275df-880d-4de0-9384-bcafa0f13d72/content). Because the environmental regulations, capital costs, and technological know-how required for chemical separation were prohibitive in the West, these miners relied on Chinese state-owned enterprises as their primary off-takers (https://repository.mines.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/cc3275df-880d-4de0-9384-bcafa0f13d72/content).

This dynamic left Western firms trapped at the lowest-margin tier of the value chain, while exposing the entire downstream ecosystem to supply shocks. To mitigate this, a new business model is currently being forged: the state-subsidized vertically integrated producer. Governments are utilizing defense appropriations and direct loans to underwrite the massive capital expenditures required to build domestic separation and metallization facilities, effectively de-risking the business model for private enterprises attempting to establish non-Chinese supply chains (https://mpmaterials.com/news/mp-materials-announces-transformational-public-private-partnership-with-the-department-of-defense-to-accelerate-u-s-rare-earth-magnet-independence/).

## 3. Demand Landscape

If the supply landscape is defined by its artificial constriction, the demand landscape is characterized by an unprecedented, structurally inevitable expansion. While rare earths have long been used in traditional industrial applications such as petroleum refining, glass additives, and polishing, these legacy end-uses are rapidly being eclipsed by the explosive demand generated by the energy transition, advanced military technology, and the proliferation of artificial intelligence hardware (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf).

*   The global data center market is forecast to grow from $300–$350 billion in 2024 to $600–$1,000 billion by 2030, presenting an enormous material footprint (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom).
*   By 2030, AI data centers alone could account for 2 to 3 percent of global demand for key critical minerals including rare earth elements (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom).
*   Power demand from AI data centers could rise by 165 percent by 2030, necessitating massive upgrades to global power grids that are highly dependent on magnetic components (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom).
*   The estimated leading global end use for rare earth elements in 2025 was the manufacturing of permanent magnets, specifically NdFeB (Neodymium-Iron-Boron) magnets (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-rare-earths.pdf).
*   Neodymium-Iron-Boron (NdFeB) magnets are the world's strongest commercially available permanent magnets, utilizing neodymium to provide raw magnetic strength and boron to stabilize the iron structure (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom).
*   Each modern electric vehicle requires approximately 1 to 2 kilograms of NdFeB magnets for its traction motors (https://www.procurementresource.com/reports/praseodymium-manufacturing-plant-project-report).

The demand profile is increasingly bimodal, splitting between light rare earths (such as neodymium and praseodymium, collectively known as NdPr) used for standard high-performance magnets, and heavy rare earths (such as terbium and dysprosium) required for applications that operate under extreme stress and high temperatures (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom). In defense applications, for example, precision-guided munitions and advanced aerospace platforms rely heavily on components featuring NdFeB permanent magnets (https://www.csis.org/analysis/consequences-chinas-new-rare-earths-export-restrictions). These weapons systems require magnets that will not lose their polarity or structural integrity when exposed to the massive thermal output of a jet engine or a missile launch sequence. Dysprosium is added to the NdFeB alloy specifically to improve magnet performance and heat resistance at high temperatures, making it a non-negotiable input for modern warfighting capabilities (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom).

Similarly, the AI hardware sector has emerged as a voracious consumer of these critical minerals. The physical data centers required to train and run generative algorithms require vast arrays of hard disk drives (HDDs) for mass data storage. Hard disk drives depend heavily on neodymium, praseodymium, and dysprosium, which are prized for their magnetic properties that help the actuator arms precisely read and write data across the platters (https://www.westerndigital.com/company/newsroom/press-releases/2025/2025-04-17-at-scale-hard-disk-drive-rare-earth-material-capture-program-launched).

To ground this in physical reality, dysprosium is being utilized at the bleeding edge of AI data center storage architecture. Researchers have developed a new synthetic magnetic molecule, named "1-Dy", which utilizes a single atom of dysprosium located between two nitrogen atoms (https://newatlas.com/technology/dysprosium-molecule-100x-storage/).

*   The 1-Dy molecule maintains its magnetic memory (hysteresis) up to 100 Kelvin (-173 °C), a significant thermal improvement over previous single-molecule magnets (https://newatlas.com/technology/dysprosium-molecule-100x-storage/).
*   This specific dysprosium molecular architecture is expected to enable new technologies capable of storing an unprecedented 3 terabytes of data per square centimeter (https://newatlas.com/technology/dysprosium-molecule-100x-storage/).
*   This hyper-dense storage capability could allow a hard drive the size of a postage stamp to store 100 times more digital data than current technologies, which is directly geared toward massive AI facilities like those operated by Google (https://newatlas.com/technology/dysprosium-molecule-100x-storage/).

Recognizing this immense material demand, major technology firms are actively attempting to secure their own secondary supply chains. In April 2025, Microsoft announced a collaboration with Western Digital to "mine" obsolete HDDs from Microsoft data centers, piloting an acid-free dissolution recycling (ADR) technology to extract neodymium, praseodymium, and dysprosium directly from server waste to insulate themselves from Chinese export controls (https://trellis.net/article/microsoft-mining-hard-drives-for-rare-earths/).

### Growth-Driver Analysis

The demand growth trajectory is alarming for end-users relying on the current constrained supply chain. The International Energy Agency (IEA) models point to a structural necessity for massive capital expenditure just to maintain current growth rates.

*   Refining capacity expansions account for nearly half of the total investment needs required to close impending rare earth supply gaps (https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/88d2b060-4c5b-46cc-8727-c60576cb937d/RareearthelementsPathwaystosecureanddiversifiedsupplychains.pdf).
*   Magnet manufacturing infrastructure represents approximately one-third of the total required investment to stabilize the global supply chain (https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/88d2b060-4c5b-46cc-8727-c60576cb937d/RareearthelementsPathwaystosecureanddiversifiedsupplychains.pdf).
*   The global demand for vital critical minerals like neodymium and dysprosium is being structurally driven into a "critical mineral supercycle" by compounding pressures from EVs, defense, and AI data centers (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom).
*   Industry forecasts suggest total mineral demand related directly to data centers could rise by 20 to 72 percent by 2050 (https://amandavandyke.substack.com/p/the-mineral-backbone-of-the-ai-boom).

The divergence in demand between base elements and specific thermal additives underscores a crucial nuance in the demand landscape: the technologies of the future—specifically high-heat defense applications and hyper-dense AI data centers—disproportionately rely on the exact heavy rare earth elements (dysprosium and terbium) that are most thoroughly monopolized by adversarial supply chains.

### China Minmetals and the China Rare Earth Group

The most consequential market participant in the global critical minerals ecosystem is not a single corporation, but a massive state-orchestrated conglomerate. In late 2021, the Chinese government executed a historic consolidation of its domestic rare earth sector to maximize its global pricing power and strategic leverage (https://repository.mines.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/cc3275df-880d-4de0-9384-bcafa0f13d72/content).

On December 23, 2021, the China Rare Earth Group Co. Ltd was formally established in Ganzhou, Jiangxi Province, operating as a state-owned enterprise directly supervised by China's national state assets regulator (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/). This newly launched mega-firm was created through the aggressive merger of several top-tier industrial producers, integrating the rare earth units of three of the "Big Six" state-owned enterprises that historically dominated the industry: Aluminum Corporation of China (CHALCO), China Minmetals Corporation, and Ganzhou Rare Earth Group Co. (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/).

*   The State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (SASAC) holds a leading 31.21 percent share ratio in the newly formed China Rare Earth Group (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/).
*   Aluminum Corporation of China (CHALCO) holds a 20.33 percent share ratio in the merged conglomerate (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/).
*   China Minmetals Corporation holds an equal 20.33 percent share ratio in the new state-owned enterprise (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/).
*   Ganzhou Rare Earth Group Co., Ltd holds a 20.33 percent share ratio in the combined mega-firm (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/).
*   Alongside mining giants, two research organizations—China Iron & Steel Research Institute Group and Grinm Group Corporation Ltd—each hold a 3.9 percent share ratio to consolidate technological and intellectual property dominance (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/).

The scale of this entity is staggering. Based on data from the time of its formation, the China Rare Earth Group controls an estimated 52,719 metric tons of mining quotas, representing 31 percent of China's national total (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/). Even more critically, the firm commands 47,129 metric tons of smelting and separation quotas, equating to 29 percent of the national total (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/). Under its configuration, this single state-controlled entity manages an estimated 62 percent of China's heavy rare earth supplies (https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/). This immense concentration grants the conglomerate unprecedented authority to dictate global price floors for vital elements like dysprosium and terbium.

### MP Materials

In the United States, the burden of establishing a resilient domestic supply chain falls disproportionately on MP Materials Corp. Headquartered in Las Vegas, Nevada, MP Materials owns and operates the Mountain Pass production site in California, which currently stands as North America's only active and scaled rare earth mining facility (https://mpmaterials.com/news/mp-materials-announces-transformational-public-private-partnership-with-the-department-of-defense-to-accelerate-u-s-rare-earth-magnet-independence/).

To break the U.S. reliance on Chinese processing, the U.S. Department of Defense has engaged in a transformational, multi-tiered public-private partnership with MP Materials.

*   In July 2025, the DoD executed a massive equity intervention by purchasing $400 million of a newly created series of MP Materials' convertible preferred stock (https://mpmaterials.com/news/mp-materials-announces-transformational-public-private-partnership-with-the-department-of-defense-to-accelerate-u-s-rare-earth-magnet-independence/).
*   Upon conversion and exercise, this purchase makes the Defense Department a 15 percent shareholder in the corporation (https://mpmaterials.com/news/mp-materials-announces-transformational-public-private-partnership-with-the-department-of-defense-to-accelerate-u-s-rare-earth-magnet-independence/).
*   To support separation expansion, the DoD agreed to provide a $150 million direct loan for a 12-year period to expand heavy rare-earths separation capabilities at Mountain Pass (https://payneinstitute.mines.edu/explainer-on-the-mp-materials-department-of-defense-partnership/).
*   The DoD committed to purchasing 7,000 metric tons per year for 10 years of rare-earth magnets from MP Materials to meet defense needs and supply commercial markets (https://payneinstitute.mines.edu/explainer-on-the-mp-materials-department-of-defense-partnership/).

The most revolutionary aspect of this partnership is the establishment of a government-backed price floor, specifically engineered to insulate the domestic producer from being undercut by Chinese state dumping. The DoD established a 10-year agreement with MP Materials that guarantees a minimum price floor of $110 per kilogram for all NdPr products stockpiled or sold by the company (https://fas.org/publication/unpacking-dod-and-mp-partnership/).

Mechanically, this price floor operates as a modified "Contract for Difference" (CfD) (https://fas.org/publication/unpacking-dod-and-mp-partnership/). If the global market price of NdPr falls below the $110 floor—for example, dropping to $60 per kilogram—the DoD is legally obligated to pay MP Materials the $50 difference out of taxpayer funds for every kilogram of material produced (https://fas.org/publication/unpacking-dod-and-mp-partnership/). At a market price of $60/kg, covering a 7,000 MT capacity would cost the DoD roughly $300 million per year (https://fas.org/publication/unpacking-dod-and-mp-partnership/). Conversely, the agreement includes a profit-sharing mechanism to protect the government if prices soar: if MP Materials generates profits above a $140 million EBITDA threshold from commercial sales, the first $30 million goes to the DoD, after which remaining profits are shared 50/50 between the DoD and the company (https://fas.org/publication/unpacking-dod-and-mp-partnership/).

### Lynas Rare Earths

While MP Materials anchors the North American response, Australia's Lynas Rare Earths serves as the crucial international pillar of the allied critical minerals strategy. Lynas holds the distinction of being the world's only commercial-scale processor of rare earths located outside of China (https://wcsecure.weblink.com.au/pdf/LYC/02692868.pdf). The company sources its raw feedstock from the highly productive Mt Weld deposit in Western Australia (https://wcsecure.weblink.com.au/pdf/LYC/02692868.pdf).

Recognizing the strategic necessity of a diversified processing footprint, the U.S. Department of Defense has heavily funded Lynas to establish physical separation capabilities on American soil.

*   Through its wholly-owned subsidiary Lynas USA LLC, the company is establishing rare earth separation capacity on a 149-acre greenfield site in Seadrift, Texas (https://wcsecure.weblink.com.au/pdf/LYC/02692868.pdf).
*   In July 2026, Lynas announced an updated contract with the DoD that significantly increased the U.S. government's capital contribution for the heavy rare earth project to approximately $258 million (https://wcsecure.weblink.com.au/pdf/LYC/02692868.pdf).
*   This arrangement is structured as an expenditure-based contract under which all of Lynas' properly allocable construction costs will be reimbursed by the U.S. government (https://www.mining-technology.com/news/lynas-updated-contract-heavy-rare/).
*   The contract is sponsored by the U.S. Government's Industrial Base Analysis and Sustainment (IBAS) program to specifically support the development of a resilient domestic supply chain (https://wcsecure.weblink.com.au/pdf/LYC/02692868.pdf).
*   The Texas facility is targeted to be fully operational in the financial year 2026, receiving mixed feedstock shipped directly from Western Australia for final separation in the United States (https://wcsecure.weblink.com.au/pdf/LYC/02692868.pdf).

## 5. Geopolitical And Macro Risk

The overarching geopolitical risk within the rare earth supply chain is the explicit weaponization of market dominance to achieve foreign policy objectives. Because the processing and refinement of these minerals is heavily localized, the supply chain is highly brittle and susceptible to rapid, catastrophic disruption based on sudden regulatory changes or retaliatory trade measures.

This risk profile materialized acutely during the geopolitical friction of 2024 and 2025. By December 2024, China had already applied explicit pressure to the United States by barring exports of several controlled minerals and tightening scrutiny of graphite shipments (https://anderseninstitute.org/chinas-export-control-architecture-and-its-use-of-critical-minerals-as-strategic-pressure-points/). This evolved significantly in 2025, driven by retaliatory trade measures. In response to U.S. President Donald Trump's tariff announcements on Chinese products, Beijing utilized its dominant market position to effectively halt the export of vital rare earth metals to the West (https://www.csis.org/analysis/consequences-chinas-new-rare-earths-export-restrictions).

*   On April 4, 2025, China imposed the aforementioned export controls on seven heavy rare earths and magnets (https://www.csis.org/analysis/consequences-chinas-new-rare-earths-export-restrictions).
*   Chinese rare earth magnet exports fell by about three‑quarters in the two months immediately following the implementation of the new controls (https://orfamerica.org/orf-america-comments/chinas-rare-earth-export-restrictions-triggered-diversification).
*   Exporters halted shipments overnight as they awaited national-security-based approvals from Beijing, placing Western manufacturers in extreme peril (https://orfamerica.org/orf-america-comments/chinas-rare-earth-export-restrictions-triggered-diversification).

The escalation continued late into the year. On October 9, 2025, China introduced its most comprehensive export restrictions to date, which notably mirrored the extraterritorial elements of the U.S. export control regime (https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/china-intensifies-export-controls-over-rare-earths-and-related-technologies-102lpyn).

*   The October 2025 controls applied extraterritorial reach; any foreign-made product containing 0.1 percent or more of Chinese-origin rare earths now requires a Chinese export license (https://anderseninstitute.org/chinas-export-control-architecture-and-its-use-of-critical-minerals-as-strategic-pressure-points/).
*   Furthermore, any product manufactured globally using Chinese rare-earth processing technologies is now subject to Beijing's licensing authority, expanding Chinese regulatory authority across completely globalized supply chains (https://anderseninstitute.org/chinas-export-control-architecture-and-its-use-of-critical-minerals-as-strategic-pressure-points/).
*   The expanded controls heavily restricted the export of vital rare earth production equipment, including centrifugal extraction equipment, specialized kilns, and permanent magnet forming presses, preventing Western nations from simply importing Chinese processing hardware (https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/china-intensifies-export-controls-over-rare-earths-and-related-technologies-102lpyn).

### Risk-Analysis

The macro risk environment is therefore defined by an ongoing game of economic attrition. The U.S. is attempting to decouple from a highly efficient, deeply entrenched state monopoly by subsidizing domestic production, but the timeline for this decoupling involves profound systemic risk. Even if raw minerals are mined successfully in California or Western Australia, turning them into high-purity metals remains a capital-intensive, time-consuming process. This processing bottleneck means that any nation attempting to overtake or bypass China's dominance faces an inescapable delay of several years as specialized infrastructure is designed, permitted, and built.

During this transitional period—which is expected to last through the late 2020s—the AI and defense hardware sectors remain entirely exposed to Beijing's regulatory whims. While the U.S. government is actively mitigating this through Defense Production Act funding, the immediate vulnerability cannot be papered over. China does not face these internal mineral bottlenecks for its own robotics, autonomous vehicle, and AI development, granting its domestic technology sectors a massive, unrestricted structural advantage while Western peers scramble for rationed materials.

## 6. Price Outlook

The convergence of explosive demand for AI and defense hardware with a deeply constricted, weaponized supply chain has generated extreme volatility in rare earth pricing. Following the geopolitical shocks of 2025 and early 2026, the market entered a period of structurally elevated pricing, particularly concerning the heavy rare earth elements utilized in high-performance permanent magnets.

To analyze the price outlook accurately, one must differentiate between the light rare earth complex (NdPr) and the heavy rare earth additives (terbium and dysprosium). The light rare earth complex experienced a highly volatile first half of 2026. After a massive 105 percent rally in the first quarter of 2026, the NdPr complex suffered a steep price correction downward throughout April and May (https://rare-earth-mining.com/rare-earth-market-june-2026/).

This April/May correction was not caused by oversupply, but was mechanically driven by a severe, temporary demand shock resulting directly from China's export restrictions. Because export volumes of these rare earth elements and permanent magnets fell so sharply in April and May, Western automakers were left entirely starved of inputs; consequently, automakers in the United States, Europe, and Japan were forced to drastically cut utilization rates or temporarily shut down their electric vehicle production lines altogether (https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/88d2b060-4c5b-46cc-8727-c60576cb937d/RareearthelementsPathwaystosecureanddiversifiedsupplychains.pdf). This forced shutdown effectively destroyed immediate spot demand for raw materials at the factory gates, causing prices to correct downwards until export licenses were eventually granted and the automotive production lines roared back to life, triggering a V-shaped recovery in June (https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/88d2b060-4c5b-46cc-8727-c60576cb937d/RareearthelementsPathwaystosecureanddiversifiedsupplychains.pdf).

*   By April 2026, domestic Chinese NdPr oxide prices had rebounded to approximately 107–108 USD/kg (https://www.moomoo.com/community/feed/weekly-strategy-report-u-s-rare-earths-2026-mp-has-116522130735909).
*   Following the end of the factory demand-shock correction, the NdPr alloy benchmark rose 10.0 percent month-on-month in June to hit $109.55 per kilogram (https://rare-earth-mining.com/rare-earth-market-june-2026/).
*   During this June 2026 recovery phase, terbium oxide prices surged an incredible 22.8 percent in a single month to reach $969.69 per kilogram (https://rare-earth-mining.com/rare-earth-market-june-2026/).
*   In contrast, dysprosium acted as an outlier in June 2026, softening 5.5 percent to $208.68 per kilogram, marking a divergence from its co-produced sister element terbium (https://rare-earth-mining.com/rare-earth-market-june-2026/).
*   Meanwhile, germanium—a critical semiconductor input facing its own export curbs—reached a 2026 high of $2,673.66 per kilogram inside China (https://rare-earth-mining.com/rare-earth-market-june-2026/).

However, the true pricing shocks occurred outside of China's borders. Because physical availability outside of China remains structurally thin, any reduction in Chinese domestic export license approvals immediately forces global ex-China prices violently upward, creating a bifurcated global market.

*   Following the initial export controls, prices outside of China surged dramatically; Western magnet manufacturers have reportedly been forced to pay up to three to five times more for inputs than they did prior to the implementation of the trade restrictions (https://www.mining.com/mp-materials-lands-multi-billion-pentagon-deal/).
*   When export licenses were finally granted after the April/May supply freeze, a massive and lasting price premium remained embedded in the market for any magnets produced outside of China, reflecting the exorbitant cost of security considerations (https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/88d2b060-4c5b-46cc-8727-c60576cb937d/RareearthelementsPathwaystosecureanddiversifiedsupplychains.pdf).

Corporate buyers must navigate this environment with extreme caution. Published benchmark assessments often reflect domestic Chinese liquidity, while actual acquisition costs for Western AI hardware and defense firms are dependent on highly confidential bilateral contracts and are subject to massive risk premiums. The establishment of artificial benchmark floors, such as the U.S. Department of Defense's guaranteed $110 per kilogram floor for MP Materials' NdPr products, further distorts the free-market pricing mechanism. It transforms the rare earth sector from a traditional commodity market into a heavily managed, strategic quasi-utility where price discovery is dictated by national security imperatives rather than fundamental supply and demand.

As long as the foundational inputs for physical AI arrays and defense platforms remain bottled within a geopolitical adversary's processing infrastructure, the forward price outlook remains aggressively skewed to the upside. The market will remain entirely dependent on the speed and volume of Chinese export license approvals, dominated continually by the latent threat of total embargos until sufficient, localized Western separation capacity comes fully online toward the end of the decade.
