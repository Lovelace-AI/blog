# Strategic Alternatives Report: Cobalt Supply Chain and EV Battery Evolution

## 1. Market Overview

The global market for cobalt is currently situated at a critical inflection point, driven by the rapid evolution of electric vehicle (EV) battery chemistries and an increasing emphasis on supply chain sustainability. The overarching theme characterizing the current landscape is a transition from traditional lithium-ion frameworks toward next-generation energy storage solutions and circular economy models.

A primary driver of this market shift is the aggressive research and development into alternative battery technologies designed to reduce reliance on critical minerals or enhance performance metrics. LG Energy Solution has been at the forefront of this evolution, showcasing artificial intelligence (AI)-powered energy storage systems (ESS), robotics batteries, and drone batteries [2]. More critically for the cobalt outlook, the industry is pursuing the commercialization of solid-state, lithium-metal, and sodium batteries [2, 5]. Solid-state batteries, in particular, are expected to revolutionize battery design by offering longer lifespans, enhanced safety profiles, and higher energy densities [5]. The emergence of sodium-ion batteries represents a significant strategic pivot; this market is projected to experience rapid and strong growth through 2033, catalyzed by regulatory shifts and evolving customer expectations regarding efficiency and sustainability [32].

Parallel to the development of new chemistries is the rise of the lithium-ion battery recycling market. This sector is becoming a material component of the supply chain as producers seek to mitigate primary extraction risks. Glencore has been identified as one of the leading players in this space, alongside Umicore, Cirba Solutions, Contemporary Amperex Technology Co., Ltd, and Redwood Materials, Inc. [3]. The ability to recover cobalt through recycling serves as a hedge against the geopolitical volatility associated with primary ore extraction.

The financial health of major battery manufacturers provides a proxy for broader market demand. LG Energy Solution's recent performance illustrates the volatility inherent in this transition. In the full-year 2025 period, the company reported consolidated revenue of KRW 23.7 trillion and an operating profit of KRW 1.3 trillion [11]. However, by the first quarter of 2026, while revenue increased slightly on a quarter-on-quarter basis to KRW 6.6 trillion—driven by stable demand for 46-series batteries—the company reported an operating loss of KRW 207.8 billion [27]. This divergence suggests that while top-line demand for battery cells remains resilient, the cost structures and margin pressures associated with scaling new technologies are significant.

## 2. Supply Landscape

The supply landscape for cobalt is characterized by extreme geographic concentration and increasing state intervention, particularly within the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). This concentration creates a systemic risk for downstream users like Tesla and LG Energy Solution, who must navigate both geopolitical instability and the strategic ambitions of sovereign entities.

### 2.1 DRC Dominance and State Intervention
The DRC remains the central pillar of global cobalt supply, but the nature of this supply is shifting toward greater state control. The Democratic Republic of Congo has established a strategic reserve for cobalt, coltan, and germanium [41]. This reserve is managed by ARECOMS, which is authorized to acquire, hold, and market these minerals specifically to influence global prices and strengthen the nation's economic position [41]. 

Furthermore, the Congolese government has implemented strict export quota systems. In March 2026, the government warned mining companies that any cobalt export quota volumes not shipped within specified deadlines—such as Q4 2025 quotas by April 30, 2026, and Q1 2026 quotas by end-June 2026—would be forfeited and transferred to the government's own strategic reserve [37]. This mechanism allows the state to effectively tighten global supply at will, introducing a layer of sovereign risk that can lead to sudden price spikes or supply shortages.

### 2.2 Corporate Strategic Positioning
Major mining entities are responding to this concentration by forming strategic partnerships to secure "reliable flows" of critical minerals [21]. Glencore has entered into a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) with the US-backed Orion Critical Mineral Consortium [21]. This partnership is designed to expand and develop key assets, including Mutanda Mining and Kamoto Copper Company, while pursuing additional critical mineral projects across the DRC and the broader African continent [40]. By partnering with a US-backed consortium, Glencore is attempting to institutionalize its access to DRC ores and mitigate the risks of unilateral state action.

In North America, supply chain resilience is being bolstered through domestic processing capacity. Glencore's Horne smelter serves as a critical asset for the North American supply chain [31]. The facility provides domestic smelting capacity for copper and produces refined metal along with essential byproducts, including sulphuric acid and other critical minerals [31]. This reduces the reliance on overseas refining, although the primary ore source remains concentrated in regions like the DRC.

### 2.3 Operational Risks in Primary Extraction
The supply of cobalt often occurs as a byproduct of copper and gold mining, meaning that disruptions in those sectors directly impact cobalt availability. Freeport-McMoRan’s operations at the Grasberg mine in Indonesia illustrate these vulnerabilities. The mine suffered a fatal mudslide, leading to a force majeure and the closure of the Grasberg Block Cave portion [25]. While production is expected to fully resume by the end of 2027 [33], the event caused immediate impacts on copper supply and highlighted the fragility of large-scale mining operations [25].

Additionally, technical risks such as water management are becoming more prominent. Experts have identified the water table level head as a critical and underappreciated risk to safety and long-term performance at the Grasberg Block Cave, noting that current solutions are insufficient [13]. Such operational failures can lead to prolonged shutdowns, further tightening the supply of byproduct minerals.

## 3. Demand Landscape

Demand for cobalt is currently caught in a tension between the growth of the broader EV and energy storage market and the technological drive to eliminate cobalt from battery chemistries entirely.

### 3.1 Growth Drivers: EVs and Grid-Scale Storage
The primary demand driver remains the proliferation of battery electric vehicles (BEVs) and stationary energy storage devices [56]. Tesla, as a dominant player in both BEVs and grid-scale storage, continues to expand its infrastructure. A notable example is the planned $4.3 billion battery cell plant in Lansing, Michigan, developed in partnership with LG Energy Solution [15]. This facility, set to begin production in 2027, will produce batteries specifically for Tesla's Megapack 3 systems [15].

The broader market for high-performance batteries is also expanding. The global fast-charging lithium-ion battery market is projected to grow from USD 55.23 billion in 2025 to USD 132.67 billion by 2032, representing a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 13.5% [17]. This growth suggests a sustained need for high-energy-density materials, which historically has favored cobalt-bearing chemistries.

### 3.2 The Substitution Threat: Sodium and Solid-State
The most significant long-term risk to cobalt demand is the shift toward "cobalt-free" or reduced-cobalt chemistries. As noted in the market overview, sodium-ion batteries are projected for rapid growth through 2033 [32]. Because sodium is abundant and does not require the complex, high-risk supply chains associated with cobalt, its adoption would materially erode the demand base for cobalt ores.

Similarly, the development of all-solid-state batteries aims to offer higher energy densities and enhanced safety [5]. While some solid-state designs may still utilize transition metals, the overarching industry trend is toward diversification to avoid the "bottleneck" created by cobalt's supply concentration in the DRC.

### 3.3 Vertical Integration and Offtake Strategies
To manage these demand risks and ensure supply security, battery manufacturers are moving toward direct offtake agreements with refiners. LG Energy Solution has entered into a strategic supply agreement with Electra to secure battery-grade cobalt sulfate [30]. Under this arrangement, LG Energy Solution is committed to approximately 60% of the refinery's planned production for up to six years [30]. This strategy allows LG Energy Solution to lock in volumes and potentially stabilize costs, even as they research alternative chemistries.

## 4. Key Producers And Market Participants

The cobalt market is dominated by a small number of diversified miners and battery technology firms, creating an oligopolistic structure where the actions of a few entities can shift global availability.

### 4.1 Glencore: The Integrated Powerhouse
Glencore occupies a unique position as both a primary producer in the DRC and a leader in the recycling market [3, 21]. Its business model is predicated on vertical integration and strategic partnerships. By partnering with the Orion Critical Mineral Consortium to develop Mutanda Mining and Kamoto Copper Company, Glencore secures its upstream access to cobalt ores [40]. Simultaneously, its identification as a leading player in lithium-ion battery recycling allows it to capture value at the end of the battery lifecycle [3]. This dual approach mitigates the risk of primary ore shortages.

### 4.2 Freeport-McMoRan: The Byproduct Producer
Freeport-McMoRan operates primarily as a copper and gold producer, with cobalt often appearing as a byproduct. Its strategic focus is on maximizing recovery from existing assets and expanding brownfield sites. For instance, the company is advancing engineering evaluations for an expansion at its Bagdad mine in Arizona, which aims to double production [42]. 

However, Freeport-McMoRan's contribution to the critical minerals market is subject to significant operational volatility. The Grasberg mine's mudslide and subsequent force majeure demonstrate how a single event can disrupt the supply of byproduct minerals [25]. Furthermore, the company is investing in "Green Mining" through AI and machine learning for geological modeling and predictive maintenance to optimize extraction efficiency [34].

### 4.3 LG Energy Solution: The Downstream Anchor
LG Energy Solution acts as the primary demand aggregator. Its strategy involves a mix of massive capital expenditure (such as the $4.3 billion plant with Tesla) and strategic offtakes (such as the agreement with Electra) [15, 30]. The company's financial volatility—moving from a profit in 2025 to an operating loss in Q1 2026—reflects the high cost of maintaining leadership in a rapidly evolving technological environment [11, 27].

## 5. Geopolitical And Macro Risk

The cobalt supply chain is one of the most geopolitically sensitive in the critical minerals sector, with risks spanning from sovereign state manipulation to international regulatory mandates.

### 5.1 Sovereign Risk and the "DRC Bottleneck"
As established, the DRC's creation of a strategic reserve managed by ARECOMS allows the state to act as a market maker [41]. The ability of the government to seize unused export quotas from private miners transforms cobalt supply into a political tool [37]. For investors and producers, this means that "proven reserves" are not equivalent to "accessible supply," as the sovereign entity can restrict flows to influence global prices.

### 5.2 Regulatory and ESG Pressures
The European Union is introducing stringent mandates that will impact how cobalt is sourced. The EU's Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive will mandate that large companies operating within the EU address human rights and environmental issues throughout their supply chains [4]. This includes ensuring a "living income" for workers in the supply chain [4]. Given the historical associations of DRC cobalt mining with human rights concerns, this directive creates a significant compliance risk. Companies like LG Energy Solution, which operate globally, face substantial penalties if they cannot verify the ethical provenance of their cobalt [4].

### 5.3 Macroeconomic and Energy Volatility
Mining operations are highly sensitive to energy costs. Freeport-McMoRan has reported renewed cost pressures due to volatility in diesel prices following conflicts in Iran [16]. In March alone, the rise in diesel prices was estimated to result in a $500 million annualized cost headwind for the company [16]. Similar pressures have been noted regarding sulfuric acid prices [49]. 

These macro risks are compounded by broader financial market instability. South Korean markets—where LG Energy Solution is listed—have experienced extreme volatility, including record one-day plunges in the Kospi and Kosdaq indices driven by Middle East tensions [18, 45]. Such volatility can restrict access to capital for the massive investments required to build new battery plants or develop alternative chemistries.

### 5.4 Business Model Risk Analysis
From an investment banking perspective, the business models of these participants are exposed to different risk vectors:
*   **Mining Companies (Glencore/Freeport):** Exposed to "Input Cost Inflation" (diesel) and "Sovereign Seizure" (DRC quotas). Their margins are squeezed when energy prices rise while state-controlled quotas limit their ability to increase volume.
*   **Battery Manufacturers (LG Energy Solution):** Exposed to "Technology Obsolescence." The investment in a $4.3 billion plant [15] is a bet on current chemistries; if sodium-ion batteries achieve mass adoption faster than expected [32], these assets could become stranded.
*   **OEMs (Tesla):** Exposed to "Systemic Bottlenecks." While cobalt is a risk, Tesla's leadership has identified other limiting factors, such as chip production, which may require the construction of a domestic "Terafab" to mitigate growth constraints [44].

## 6. Price Outlook

While specific quantitative price forecasts for cobalt are not provided in the evidence, a qualitative outlook can be synthesized by analyzing the intersection of supply constraints and demand substitution.

### 6.1 Bullish Factors (Price Support)
The primary support for cobalt prices is the increasing state control over supply in the DRC [41]. The establishment of ARECOMS and the forfeiture of export quotas [37] create a structural floor under prices by limiting the amount of cobalt that can reach the open market. Furthermore, the continued growth of grid-scale storage projects—such as Tesla's Megapack 3—ensures a baseline level of demand for high-capacity batteries [15]. The projected CAGR of 13.5% for fast-charging lithium-ion batteries through 2032 also suggests that high-performance applications will continue to require cobalt in the medium term [17].

### 6.2 Bearish Factors (Price Pressure)
The most potent downward pressure on prices is the acceleration of chemistry evolution. The "rapid and strong growth" projected for sodium-ion batteries through 2033 represents a direct threat to cobalt's market share [32]. As manufacturers like LG Energy Solution successfully commercialize solid-state and sodium alternatives [2, 5], the strategic importance of cobalt will diminish.

Additionally, the rise of the recycling market—led by Glencore and others [3]—introduces a secondary supply stream that is not subject to DRC sovereign risk or export quotas. As the volume of end-of-life EV batteries increases, recycled cobalt will likely act as a price stabilizer, preventing extreme spikes caused by primary supply disruptions.

### 6.3 Synthesis and Investment View
The outlook for cobalt is one of **increasing volatility with a long-term downward trend in strategic criticality**. In the short term, prices are likely to be driven by "shocks"—specifically DRC quota changes [37] and geopolitical conflicts impacting energy costs [16]. However, the long-term trajectory is defined by substitution.

The investment thesis for cobalt-exposed assets should shift from a "growth" play to a "risk management" play. The most resilient participants are those who:
1.  **Diversify Chemistries:** Companies like LG Energy Solution that are simultaneously investing in sodium and solid-state batteries [2, 5].
2.  **Integrate Recycling:** Entities like Glencore that can pivot from primary extraction to urban mining (recycling) [3].
3.  **Secure Direct Offtakes:** Manufacturers that bypass spot market volatility through long-term refinery agreements [30].

The critical data gap remains the lack of specific cobalt price targets and a detailed breakdown of "Cobalt Ores" vs "Ferroalloy Ores" industry pricing [DATA GAP]. However, based on the evidence of sodium-ion growth [32] and DRC state intervention [41], the market is moving toward a bifurcated state: high-cost, state-controlled primary supply versus low-cost, sustainable recycled/alternative supply.

**Final Investment Conclusion:**
Investors should remain cautious regarding pure-play cobalt exposure. The combination of sovereign risk in the DRC [37, 41] and the imminent threat of sodium-ion substitution [32] creates an unfavorable risk-reward profile for long-term primary extraction. Value is instead migrating toward the "circular economy" (recycling) and the developers of next-generation, cobalt-free chemistries. The most critical decision-point for investors will be the rate of commercialization for sodium batteries; if this accelerates beyond 2033 projections, the demand collapse for cobalt could be more abrupt than currently anticipated [DATA GAP].
